Tips on Paper Selection

Choosing the right paper is one of the most important decisions in any print project — it affects color quality, feel, durability, and how your brand is perceived. Asking for "white paper" is like asking a waiter for "food" — let's get specific.

Understanding Paper Basics

Paper is described by its weight, caliper (thickness), finish, and coating. In the U.S., weight is expressed in pounds (lb.) — the weight of 500 sheets at the paper's basic size. That's why a "60 lb. Cover" is much heavier than a "60 lb. Text": they're measured at different base sheet sizes.

Paper choice touches every part of the finished piece — how colors reproduce, how readable text is, how durable the piece is, and how premium it feels in your reader's hands. Below is everything you need to choose with confidence.

The 10 Characteristics of Paper

Every paper decision comes down to ten measurable traits. Tap any card for the full explanation.

Surface Surface +

How the sheet was calendared (pressed) at the mill. More calendaring makes paper smoother and glossier but thinner, less opaque, and less bright. Surface drives how ink sits and how the piece feels.

Color Color +

White runs from stark photo-white to soft antique shades. Photo-white maximizes contrast for images; off-white reduces glare for long-form reading. Always compare sheets under standard lighting.

Brightness Brightness +

The percentage of blue light the sheet reflects (most papers fall between 60–98). Higher brightness makes colors pop; too high can cause eye strain on text-heavy documents. See the scale below.

Opacity Opacity +

How much printing shows through from the other side. Critical for double-sided pieces. Opacity rises with weight, bulk, and certain coatings.

Grain Grain Direction +

The alignment of the paper's fibers — grain long (parallel to the sheet's length) or grain short. Grain affects folding, strength, and how the finished piece lies. Always fold with the grain.

Weight Weight (Basis) +

The weight of 500 sheets at the paper's basic size. Because text and cover use different base sizes, 60 lb. cover is far heavier than 60 lb. text. Sold by the pound — key to cost control.

Caliper Caliper (Thickness) +

Measured in thousandths of an inch ("points"). .001 inch = 1 point, so 8 pt paper is .008" thick. Don't confuse caliper points with type points (font size).

Bulk Bulk +

Thickness relative to basis weight. Two sheets can share a basis weight but differ in thickness — uncalendared (less-pressed) paper is bulkier than gloss-coated at the same weight.

Size Size +

The physical dimensions of the sheet (e.g., 8.5 × 11). Knowing available sizes is essential to efficient imposition and controlling waste.

Qty Quantity +

A ream is 500 sheets. Cartons are defined by approximate weight (~150 lbs), not exact sheet count — a standard unit of sale.

Understanding Brightness

Brightness measures the percentage of light a sheet reflects, on a 0–100 scale. It's a strictly quantitative rating — unlike color, which is subjective. Higher brightness gives colors more "pop" and sharper contrast; lower brightness is easier on the eyes for long-form reading. Paper Finishes & Coatings

Coated papers have a clay or polymer layer applied to create a smoother printing surface. Uncoated papers have a natural texture that absorbs ink differently. The right choice depends on your design and audience.

Gloss Coated

High-sheen coating that makes colors pop and images vibrant. Reduces readability of text at very small sizes. Best for image-heavy marketing materials, photo books, and magazine-style pieces.

Matte Coated

A smooth, low-sheen finish that is easy to read and write on. Colors are rich but more subdued than gloss. Preferred for text-heavy pieces like reports, booklets, and direct mail.

Satin / Silk Coated

The middle ground between gloss and matte. Provides a soft sheen with good color vibrancy and readability. Increasingly popular for high-end brochures and corporate materials.

Uncoated

A natural, porous paper surface ideal for stationery, envelopes, and writing tablets. Absorbs ink more than coated stocks, which can affect color density. Provides a classic, tactile feel.

Special Finishes & Coatings

Applied after printing, these finishes add protection, texture, and visual drama to your printed piece.

Aqueous Coating

A water-based, fast-drying coating applied inline on press. Provides protection and a smooth finish. Available in gloss, satin, and matte.

UV Coating

A high-gloss varnish cured by ultraviolet light. Extremely shiny and durable — resists scuffing and fingerprints. Can be applied as a full flood coat or as a spot UV for dramatic contrast.

Soft-Touch Laminate

A velvety, suede-like matte laminate that is incredibly tactile and premium. Highly popular for luxury business cards, packaging, and cosmetic materials.

Gloss Laminate

A clear, high-gloss film bonded to the printed surface. Provides maximum color vibrancy and excellent durability.

Matte Laminate

A flat, non-reflective film that is easy to write on and resists fingerprints. Popular for booklet covers, presentation folders, and menus.

Foil Stamping

Metallic or pigmented foil transferred to paper using heat and pressure. Available in gold, silver, copper, and holographic options. Adds luxury and attention-grabbing shimmer.

Quick Selection Tips

  • For business cards: Use 100 lb. or 120 lb. cover stock. Add a soft-touch laminate or spot UV for a premium feel.
  • For brochures: 100 lb. gloss or satin text is a popular, high-quality choice. Matte text is great for text-heavy content.
  • For booklets: 100 lb. gloss text for interior pages; 100 lb. gloss cover for the cover stock.
  • For direct mail: 100 lb. gloss cover for postcards; 80 lb. text for self-mailers.
  • For presentations & proposals: Uncoated or matte-laminated stocks for a professional, sophisticated look.
  • For menus & hospitality: 14 pt. coated board or laminated stocks for durability and wipe-ability.
